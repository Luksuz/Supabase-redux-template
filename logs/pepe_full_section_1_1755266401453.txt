Ever wonder why some beliefs stick with you, even without evidence? You wake up, reach for your phone, and within three swipes you’ve seen a flawless morning routine, a skincare reel with that glassy “clean girl” glow, maybe a post from Hailey Bieber launching a lip tint that suddenly feels essential, plus a headline about someone your age selling a startup. And before your coffee cools, a quiet voice murmurs: be better, be faster, be more.

That voice isn’t just you. It’s the echo of programming that’s so constant, it’s nearly invisible. We pick it up from the jokes our families tell at dinner, the rules we learn in classrooms, the ads between highlight reels, the way strangers talk about bodies, money, love. Some of it is harmless. Some of it is helpful. And some of it becomes a script you didn’t realize you were reading from.

Psychology has a lot to say about how this happens. Solomon Asch showed that people will doubt their own eyes to agree with a group—those famous line-length experiments where confidence collapsed under social pressure. Robert Cialdini’s work on social proof explains why we take our cues from what “people like us” are doing—hotel guests reuse more towels when told most guests do. The mere-exposure effect shows that repeat something often enough and we tend to like it more. The illusory truth effect goes further: repeat something often enough and we start to believe it’s true. Put that together with an algorithm designed to serve you more of what you linger on, and you’ve got an assembly line for beliefs that feel self-made but were, in part, manufactured.

Think about success. Did you choose the metrics you’re chasing, or did they choose you? Maybe it’s the corner office, maybe it’s 100K followers, a home by 30, a jawline you can contour into a billboard. Did you sit down and decide those were your measures, or did you absorb them through report cards, performance reviews, congratulatory posts, and the slow, steady drip of “shoulds”? Culture doesn’t always shout. Often, it whispers the same message until it sounds like your own thought.

This isn’t a scolding. You’re not gullible. You’re human. Whether you grew up in a big city or a small town, whether your feed is basketball highlights or serum routines, whether your heroes wear lab coats, hoodies, or red carpet gowns, you swim in currents that nudge how you define “good,” “worthy,” “normal.” The specifics change—one person hears “real men don’t cry,” another hears “effortless beauty is the goal,” someone else hears “if you’re not hustling, you’re failing”—but the mechanism is shared.

So pause with me. What do you believe about success, love, beauty, faith, work—because you’ve tested it and found it true, and what do you believe because everyone around you seems to? Which thoughts feel like yours, and which ones were installed by default?

Hold that question. We’re about to learn how to spot the code and start rewriting it—on purpose.
